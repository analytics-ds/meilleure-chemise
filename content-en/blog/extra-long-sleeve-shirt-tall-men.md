> **Key points:**
> 1. Shirt sizing is indexed on neck circumference, never on arm length: that is the structural cause of the problem.
> 2. Extra long sleeve lines add 4 to 5 cm to the sleeve, and often as much to the back length.
> 3. Sizing up the collar to gain sleeve length is a false solution: the gain is one to two centimetres, at the cost of a shirt that is loose everywhere.
> 4. Very few brands publish the exact gap in centimetres, which makes comparison difficult without measuring first.

## Why collar size says nothing about sleeve length

A men's shirt is ordered by neck circumference: 37, 38, 39, up to 45 or more. That measurement describes the circumference of the neck, in centimetres. It carries no information about arm length.

Brand size charts therefore apply an **average sleeve length per collar size**, calculated on a statistical build. In most French charts, a collar 41 corresponds to a sleeve of roughly 64 to 65 cm measured from the centre back to the end of the cuff.

### The problem with long-limbed builds

Two men can share exactly the same collar 41 and differ by several centimetres in arm span. A man of six foot three with a slim neck will wear a collar 40 or 41 with arms that call for the sleeve of a collar 44. The standard grid does not cover that case.

The outcome is mechanical: the cuff rides up the forearm as soon as the arm bends, and disappears under the jacket sleeve instead of showing by one or two centimetres as convention requires.

## The test for sleeves that are too short

Three checks are enough, with the shirt worn and the cuff buttoned.

1. **Arm at the side**: the cuff should fall at the base of the thumb, on the wrist bone, not above it.
2. **Arm extended forward**: the sleeve should not pull back by more than a centimetre. Significant pull-back points to a misplaced armhole as much as to a short sleeve.
3. **Arm bent at 90 degrees**: this is the decisive test. If the cuff rides clearly up the forearm, the sleeve is too short.

The third test is the one most fitting-room checks skip, because they are done with the arms hanging in front of a mirror. That is precisely the position in which the fault stays invisible.

## What extra long sleeve lines actually deliver

Several shirtmakers offer a dedicated line, often identified by a suffix in the size. The important point, and a rarely explicit one, is the **actual gap in centimetres** against the standard model.

### Published gaps

| Element | Standard shirt | Extra long sleeve line |
|---|---|---|
| Sleeve length | Chart reference | + 4 cm approximately |
| Back length | Chart reference | + 5 cm approximately |
| Neck circumference | Unchanged | Unchanged |
| Chest | Unchanged | Unchanged |

Figaret documents that gap precisely on its dedicated collection: extra long sleeve shirts have "4 cm more on the sleeve and 5 cm more on the back", identified by the letter L attached to the collar size, as in 43L.

The added back length matters as much as the sleeve. A long-limbed build needs both: without the extra back length, the shirt pulls out of the trousers as soon as the torso bends, and the sleeve gain serves no purpose.

### What the dedicated line does not change

Neck circumference and chest remain those of the size ordered. That is the whole point of the approach, and what separates it from simply sizing up.

## Why sizing up the collar is a false solution

The most common reflex is to take one collar size up to gain sleeve length. The arithmetic does not hold.

In a standard chart, moving from collar 41 to collar 42 lengthens the sleeve by roughly **one to two centimetres**. The same move widens the collar by a centimetre, the chest by four to six centimetres, and pushes the shoulder seam outward.

In other words: less than half the length gained by a dedicated line, in exchange for a shirt that floats at the torso and drops at the shoulder. On a fitted shirt, the effect is immediately visible.

## The fabrics that forgive the least

A slightly short sleeve shows more on some fabrics than others.

- **Poplin and pinpoint**: tight weave, crisp hand, the cuff holds its position and the fault is obvious.
- **Oxford and basketweave**: softer grain, the sleeve relaxes slightly with wear, which softens a one-centimetre gap without correcting it.
- **Linen**: a fabric that moves a great deal, creases and shrinks in the wash. On a long-limbed build, it is safer to allow margin at purchase. The comparison between [linen or cotton shirts](/en/blog/linen-or-cotton-shirt/) covers these behavioural differences.

Shrinkage at the first wash deserves particular attention: a cotton that has not been pre-shrunk can lose one to two centimetres in length, which is enough to turn a borderline sleeve into a short one. The [cotton shirt care guide](/en/blog/cotton-shirt-care-guide/) sets out the temperatures that limit this.

## Collars and formal occasions

On formal dress, the shirt cuff should show beyond the jacket sleeve. This is a rule of proportion rather than an affectation: it marks the boundary between jacket and hand, and structures the silhouette.

A sleeve that is too short makes that impossible, whatever the rest of the outfit. For a ceremony, sleeve length is therefore settled before colour or collar: the guide on [what shirt to wear for a wedding](/en/blog/what-shirt-to-wear-wedding-men/) covers the remaining criteria once that constraint is cleared.

Collars without points, such as the [mandarin collar](/en/blog/mandarin-collar-shirt-guide/), change nothing about sleeve length but alter the overall reading of the silhouette, which they visually lengthen.

## Where to find extra long sleeve shirts

The market remains narrow. Shirtmakers that maintain a dedicated line are a minority, and the depth of range is clearly lower than in standard collections.

In France, Figaret offers the line documented above. Café Coton also maintains an [extra long sleeve collection](https://www.cafecoton.com/fr/grandes-longueurs), holding 21 references in stock as of 29 September 2026, on fitted models in basketweave, denim and stripes, across collar sizes 37 to 42.

The point to watch at purchase is the same everywhere: check that the gap in centimetres is stated. Without it, the only reliable method is to measure a shirt that fits, from the centre back to the end of the cuff, and compare that figure with the seller's chart.

<summary>Should you size up the collar to gain sleeve length?</summary>

No. Going up one collar size adds only one to two centimetres of sleeve, while widening the collar, the shoulders and the chest. The result is a shirt that is loose everywhere for a marginal length gain. A dedicated extra long sleeve line answers the need far better.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Can the sleeves of an existing shirt be lengthened?</summary>

Rarely. A shirt sleeve carries no meaningful fabric reserve at the cuff, unlike trousers whose hem can be let down. The feasible alteration is the reverse: shortening a sleeve that is too long, a common and inexpensive operation.

</details>
