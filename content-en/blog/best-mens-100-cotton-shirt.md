> **In brief:**
> 1. None of the four brands surveyed offers a shirt catalogue made entirely of 100% cotton: all of them mix pure cotton models with linen, and with cotton-linen or cotton-lyocell blends.
> 2. Among compositions declared on 25 September 2026, Figaret lists 100% cotton on 214 of its 250 men's models, Café Coton on 43 pages out of a random sample of 60.
> 3. The marketing phrase "cotton shirt" on a rail is not a composition: only the stated percentage on the care label counts.
> 4. Recorded prices range from 29 to 225 euros, with medians of 110 euros at Café Coton and 145 euros at Figaret.

Searching for "the best men's 100% cotton shirt" almost always leads to a brand ranking. That is an incomplete answer, because fabric composition is not decided at brand level but at model level. The same house sells, on the same day, pure cotton poplins, linen shirts and gabardines blended with lyocell.

So we approached it from the other end: recording, from the live product pages, the composition each brand actually declares. Here is the result.

## What brands actually declare on their product pages

Survey carried out on 25 September 2026 on each brand's public product pages. The method is set out below the table.

| Brand | Basis surveyed | Models in 100% cotton | Other declared compositions | Recorded prices |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Figaret** | 250 models from the men's shirt collection | **214 (86%)** | 11 linen, 5 wool, 20 blends from 52% to 97% cotton | 125 to 225 €, median 145 € |
| **Café Coton** | 60 product pages drawn at random from 2,937 | **43 (72%)** | 7 linen, 9 cotton-linen and cotton-lyocell blends, 1 viscose | 29 to 140 €, median 110 € |
| **Bexley** | first page of the men's shirt collection | **majority of models** | linen-cotton blends at 55/45 and 70/30, one wool model | roughly 64 to 70 € |
| **Charles Tyrwhitt** | flagship non-iron models | **100% cotton** | dedicated cotton-linen filter in the catalogue | 84.95 € list, 69.95 € reduced |

**Method.** For Figaret and Café Coton, compositions were extracted automatically from each product page, which allows an exact count across the stated basis. For Bexley, the survey covers the models displayed on the first page of the collection, which gives a trend rather than an exhaustive count. For Charles Tyrwhitt, compositions do not appear on the collection page, so two representative product pages were opened individually. The figures describe the catalogues as they stood on 25 September 2026 and will shift with each season.

### What this table really shows

The most useful reading is not the ranking, it is the gap between a brand's image and its actual catalogue.

A house whose name contains the word cotton is not therefore pure cotton throughout: in the sample surveyed, close to three pages in ten declare something else. Conversely, a brand positioned on formalwear such as Figaret concentrates 86% of its range on pure cotton, simply because cotton poplin and twill are the fabrics of the business wardrobe.

In other words, composition follows the type of shirt, not the retailer. A business shirt is very likely to be 100% cotton whichever brand makes it. A summer shirt, a casual with a fluid drape or a printed piece is very likely to contain linen, lyocell or viscose, whichever brand makes it too.

## How to check a composition before buying

Three levels of information coexist, and they do not carry the same weight.

1. **The marketing wording on the rail or in the product title.** "Cotton shirt", "pure cotton", "cotton-rich": none of these is a regulated composition. They guarantee nothing.
2. **The online product page.** This is where the stated percentage should appear. Of the four brands surveyed, three display it directly in the description. A page with no percentage is not a 100% cotton page: it is a page with no information.
3. **The care label sewn into the garment.** Found in the side seam or under the collar, it carries the actual composition and prevails if it differs from the product page.

### The low-percentage blend trap

One detail often goes unnoticed: a blend at 95 or 97% cotton, of the kind found at Figaret, generally contains 3 to 5% elastane. This is not filler, it is a comfort choice on a slim cut.

That blend does change the care routine, however. Elastane tolerates heat poorly: a 60-degree wash or a tumble dry slackens the fibre and the shirt loses its shape irreversibly. The advice in our [cotton shirt care guide](/en/blog/cotton-shirt-care-guide/) applies to pure cotton, not to elastane blends, which require a 30-degree wash and flat drying.

## 100% cotton, blends and treatments: what to pick for which use

### For the office all year round

100% cotton in poplin or twill weave remains the benchmark. The tight weave gives a crisp drape, the fibre breathes, and the shirt irons without difficulty. It is the core range of all four brands surveyed, and the segment offering the widest choice.

Weight matters as much as composition. Below 100 g/m², a white poplin stays see-through over a dark tee. Between 110 and 130 g/m², the shirt holds its shape without stifling.

### For summer

This is where 100% cotton legitimately gives ground. Pure linen and cotton-linen blends from 50/50 to 70/30 wick moisture better. The two fabrics are compared in detail in our guide on [linen or cotton shirts](/en/blog/linen-or-cotton-shirt/).

Cotton gauze shirts are a third, often overlooked option: they remain pure cotton, but their airy double weave makes them markedly more breathable than a standard poplin.

### For a formal occasion

Pure cotton is the obvious choice, in fine poplin or cotton satin. This is also the context where the collar matters as much as the fabric: picking a collar to suit your face and your tie is covered in our guide on [what shirt to wear to a wedding](/en/blog/what-shirt-to-wear-wedding-men/), and in the one devoted to the [mandarin collar](/en/blog/mandarin-collar-shirt-guide/) for tieless ceremonies.

### If wrinkling is your main criterion

Two options, and they are not equivalent.

**Cotton-polyester blends** reduce creasing but trap heat and perspiration odours. They are the choice of entry-level shirts, and the one that ages worst: polyester takes on a shiny look after repeated washes.

**A non-iron treatment applied to 100% cotton**, as on the Charles Tyrwhitt flagship models recorded at 84.95 euros, is the middle option. The composition stays natural while a resin fixed to the fabric limits creasing. The treatment fades after several dozen washes, but the shirt remains pure cotton.

## Where to find 100% cotton without hunting for it

If composition outweighs every other criterion, the most efficient approach is to start from the type of shirt rather than the brand.

A collection dedicated to business shirts, at any of the four houses surveyed, will be predominantly pure cotton. You can verify that in seconds by opening two or three product pages at random within the collection, which is exactly the method we applied here at scale.

As a concrete starting point, the [Café Coton men's shirt collection](https://www.cafecoton.com/fr/chemises-homme) states the composition directly on every page, whether poplin, oxford, twill, jersey or cotton gauze, so you can decide without having to ask. That is the criterion that matters most when buying online: not that a brand is entirely pure cotton, which none is, but that it says clearly what each model is made of.

## The takeaway

The question "what is the best men's 100% cotton shirt" has no answer at brand level. It has one at model level, and that answer is written on the product page.

The real criterion for choosing a retailer is therefore not the share of pure cotton in its catalogue, but how transparently it displays its compositions. On that point, all four brands surveyed on 25 September 2026 perform well, which is far from being the norm across the wider market.
