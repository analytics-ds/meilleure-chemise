> **Key points:**
> 1. Cotton poplin remains the reference fabric for the office: a clean line under a jacket, frequent washing tolerated, simple pressing.
> 2. The Italian and classic collars cover formal settings, the button-down covers relaxed ones.
> 3. White and light blue are enough to cover almost every professional situation.
> 4. A rotation of five to seven shirts spaces out washes and delays wear at the collar and cuffs.

## The real criterion: how formal the sector is

Before fabric and before colour, it is the dress code of the sector that determines the shirt. Three registers stand clearly apart.

| Register | Typical sectors | Expectation on the shirt |
|---|---|---|
| Strictly formal | Banking, consulting, law, notarial practice | Italian or classic collar, tie, plain or fine stripe, poplin |
| Business casual | Insurance, industry, retail, public administration | Italian collar without tie, poplin or end-on-end, plain or discreet pattern |
| Relaxed | Tech, creative, communications, media | Button-down or mandarin collar, oxford, denim tolerated |

Choosing the wrong register costs more than choosing the wrong fabric. A button-down collar in a law firm stands out as much as a white poplin and tie in a product team.

## Fabrics that withstand daily wear

### Poplin

Tight weave, smooth surface, cool hand. Poplin is the reference cloth of the town shirt because it holds a clean line under a jacket and does not mark at the elbow. It is also the fabric that best accepts repeated weekly washing.

Its drawback: it creases visibly by the end of the day, particularly across the back and inside the elbows.

### Oxford

Basket weave, visible grain, heavier. Oxford creases less than poplin, which makes it an ally on long days, but its textured appearance places it in a less formal register. It sits poorly with a fine silk tie and well with chinos.

### End-on-end and pinpoint

Two middle grounds. End-on-end crosses two yarns of close colours, producing a discreet sense of depth, ideal when plain looks too flat. Pinpoint, between poplin and oxford, combines a hand close to poplin with better crease resistance.

### What to avoid at the office

Linen, despite its summer comfort, creases within the first hour of sitting and does not suit a formal register. Blends with a high polyester content hold the press better but trap heat and show sweat marks. The [comparison between linen and cotton](/en/blog/linen-or-cotton-shirt/) sets out this behaviour fabric by fabric.

## Choosing the collar for the context

The collar is the most visible part of the shirt once the jacket is closed. On its own it determines whether the outfit reads as formal or relaxed.

- **Italian collar**: spread points, room for a wide tie knot. The most versatile of the formal collars, it also works without a tie.
- **Classic collar**: narrower points, classic register, calls for a slimmer knot.
- **Button-down collar**: points fastened by a button, American in origin, relaxed by nature. Best kept for softer environments.
- **Mandarin collar**: no points, no stand-over, impossible to wear with a tie. The [mandarin collar guide](/en/blog/mandarin-collar-shirt-guide/) sets out the builds and contexts it suits.

For an office wardrobe built on five shirts, the Italian collar covers most needs without requiring a decision every morning.

## Colours that actually work

White and light blue make up the bulk of a professional wardrobe, for a simple reason: they pair with every suit and every tie, with no coordination effort.

Three reasonable extensions:

1. **Pale pink**, which stays formal and softens a grey or navy suit.
2. **Fine stripes**, blue on white or grey on white, which add relief without leaving the register.
3. **Light grey**, less common, useful for breaking a run of blues.

Saturated colours, bold checks and prints belong to the weekend wardrobe. They register too strongly to be worn several times a week in front of the same people.

## How many shirts for a week

A rotation of five to seven pieces is the point of balance. Below five, every shirt goes through the machine weekly, which prematurely wears the friction areas: the collar, the cuffs and the top button.

Above seven, the benefit becomes marginal and storage becomes an issue, since a shirt folded for too long sets fixed creases at the shoulders.

Lifespan depends above all on care. A thirty-degree wash, drying on a hanger and pressing the collar first noticeably extend the life of the cloth. The [cotton shirt care guide](/en/blog/cotton-shirt-care-guide/) covers these steps and the temperatures to respect.

## Fitted or classic cut

The fitted cut narrows the waist and follows the silhouette. It flatters a slim build but becomes uncomfortable when seated, especially across a full office day.

The classic cut leaves more ease at the chest and armholes. It is more forgiving when seated and sits better under a structured jacket.

One often overlooked criterion: length. An office shirt should stay tucked in all day, including when bending forward. Long-limbed builds run into this problem regularly, which the guide on [extra long sleeve shirts](/en/blog/extra-long-sleeve-shirt-tall-men/) addresses.

## Where to buy an office shirt

The market splits across three positions: high-street retail, at around 30 to 50 euros, specialist shirtmakers between 80 and 150 euros, and made-to-measure above that.

The price gap rests mainly on the quality of the cotton, the density of the weave and the finishing of the collar, three elements that directly determine how many washes a shirt withstands before it starts to show.

Among French shirtmakers, Café Coton offers a [business shirt range](https://www.cafecoton.com/fr/chemises-business) built on town fabrics and formal collars, with a broad catalogue covering the common collar sizes.

## Frequently asked questions

<details>
<summary>What is the best men's shirt for the office?</summary>

For daily office wear, a cotton poplin shirt in light blue or white, with an Italian or classic collar, remains the most versatile choice. Poplin holds a clean line under a jacket, withstands frequent washing and presses easily. Oxford suits less formal environments better, where a tie is not expected.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How many shirts are needed for a working week?</summary>

A rotation of five to seven shirts covers a week without emergency laundry and spaces out machine washes, which extends the life of each piece. A smaller number accelerates wear at the collar and cuffs, the first areas to show.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What shirt colour works for the office?</summary>

White and light blue cover almost every professional situation and pair with any suit. Pale pink, light grey and fine stripes widen the wardrobe without leaving the formal register. Saturated colours and bold patterns belong to softer dress codes.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Are non-iron shirts a good idea for the office?</summary>

Non-iron treatments save time but rely on a finish applied to the fabric, which fades over successive washes and slightly reduces breathability. For daily wear, an untreated cotton, well pressed, keeps a better hand over time, at the cost of a few minutes with the iron.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Can a shirt be worn without a tie at the office?</summary>

In most sectors, yes. The tie remains expected in investment banking, consulting and parts of the legal profession. Elsewhere, an Italian collar worn open at the first button is the business casual norm. The collar then needs enough structure not to collapse without a knot.

</details>
